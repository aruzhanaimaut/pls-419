PLS 419 final project: an AI answer for Kazakhstan

Қазақ قازاق Qazaq

Cyrillic, used in Kazakhstan's schools

They come home speaking Kazakh. Then school asks them to read it.

Every year about six thousand ethnic Kazakh children return to Kazakhstan with their families. Many speak Kazakh fluently and still cannot read the classroom board. This project asks why, maps what the state counts and what it doesn't, and prices an AI reading tutor called Көпір ("bridge") before deciding whether Kazakhstan should build it.

Try to read this sentence:

مەن مەكتەپكە بارامىن

This is Kazakh written in tote zhazu, the Arabic-based script used by Kazakhs in China. Many kandas children meet Cyrillic the way you just met this line.

Start with the problem
1

The problem

Kazakhstan invites ethnic Kazakhs abroad to come home. Their children arrive Kazakh enough to qualify, and not yet able to read the classroom board.

Since independence, Kazakhstan has invited ethnic Kazakhs living abroad to resettle in their historical homeland. People granted this status are now officially called kandas ("of the same blood"); the older term, oralman, has been replaced. By the end of 2025, 1.16 million people had returned [1]. The flow has not stopped: 19,178 people received the status in 2022 [6], 21,325 in 2023 [3], more than 19,700 in 2024 [4] and 16,805 in 2025 [1]. About a third are children: minors were 30.9% of kandas at the start of 2024 [3] and 34% at the start of 2026 [2]. That works out to roughly 5,700 to 6,600 children a year. That figure is my estimate, because no ministry publishes a count of kandas children.

These children are not one group. In 2022, almost two thirds of arrivals came from Uzbekistan [7]. By 2024 China had become the largest source, at 45.4% [5], and in 2025 the two were level: 44.5% from Uzbekistan and 44.1% from China [2]. Nor do the families settle where the state plans. The government pays resettlement support for moving to labour-short regions in the north, east and west, but in 2025 only 2,309 people moved under that quota [9], about 14% of the year's arrivals. In early 2023 most new kandas settled in Almaty region (27.2%), Mangystau (13.8%), Astana (11%), Almaty city (8.8%), Shymkent (7.1%) and Turkestan region (6.4%) [8]. So the pressure falls on particular schools in the south, the west and the big cities.

A 2026 study of 250 kandas pupils in grades 8 to 11 in Almaty, Astana, Mangystau and Turkistan documents what happens in those schools [13]. Many pupils spoke Kazakh better than they could read or write it. Russian was a major barrier, especially for pupils who had never been schooled in Cyrillic. They also struggled with the Cyrillic script, with study materials, with speaking up in class and with dialect differences. Their starting points depend on where they come from. Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan write their national languages in Latin script. Children from China face a sharper gap: Xinjiang's "bilingual" schools have moved instruction into Mandarin [31], and in 2017 one county in the Ili Kazakh Autonomous Prefecture banned Kazakh-language textbooks outright [30]. A child can be the most fluent Kazakh speaker in the class and its weakest Kazakh reader.

At this point the problem stops being only about teaching. The state admits these children because they are Kazakh, and repatriation is part of its nation-building story. Yet at school their Kazakh is treated as non-standard and their weak Russian as a deficit. Researchers describe the same boundary in everyday life, where locals draw "insider" and "outsider" lines around returnees [17].

When an institution welcomes people as co-ethnics, who is expected to adapt: the child, the family or the school? And what happens to belonging when the child's own Kazakh is marked as wrong?

The social-science question inside the issue

Several groups bear the problem. First are the children: their learning, their confidence and their sense of having come home. Next are the teachers, who receive them without training or materials. Finally there is the state itself, whose promise that kandas are "returning" is tested in every classroom. The problem is also changing. As the share of arrivals from China grows, so, very probably, does the share of children who have never been schooled in Cyrillic. And Kazakhstan's planned move from Cyrillic to a Latin alphabet will add a third script to the ones these children already juggle.

2

Background and data

Kazakhstan counts kandas carefully as workers and settlers. As pupils, they are almost invisible.

The main source is not the Bureau of National Statistics but the Ministry of Labour and Social Protection. Roughly once a month it publishes running totals: how many people received kandas status, their countries of origin, an age split into working age, minors and pensioners, the education level of working-age adults, and how many moved to quota regions and received support [1–5]. The Ministry of Science and Higher Education publishes the only series about young kandas. University grants under the kandas quota rose from 801 in 2023 to 1,604 in 2024, 2,138 in 2025 and 2,378 in 2026 [10], after a 2024 rule change let kandas sit the national test (UNT) in three subjects with no threshold score [11]. UNDP's 2013 survey of oralmans is the last large international study I found, and it focuses on adults [44].

The numbers show three patterns. First, the flow is steady at 17,000 to 21,000 people a year; this is not a wave that has passed. Second, the origin mix is changing fast. China rose from 8.6% of arrivals in 2022 to 44–45% in 2024 and 2025, while Uzbekistan fell from 63.5% to between 38.9% and 44.5% [7] [5] [2]. Children from China are the ones most likely to have been schooled in Mandarin, so this shift probably widens the script gap rather than closing it. Third, the share of minors is rising, from 30.9% to 34% [3] [2].

Where the data is silent

What no one publishes is the part that matters for schools: how many kandas children are enrolled, in which grades, in Kazakh- or Russian-medium classes, which script they were schooled in, how long they wait before enrolling, how they do on tests, and whether they repeat a year or drop out. I could find no breakdown of pupils by migration background from either the Bureau of National Statistics or the Ministry of Enlightenment. Even the child count has to be estimated by multiplying arrivals by the share of minors, and that age band lumps toddlers together with 17-year-olds.

Some children fall through these gaps completely. Children whose families have not yet obtained status are not counted at all. Teenagers who arrive at 15 to 17 have the least time to catch up before national exams, yet nothing records whether they finish school. The trustworthiness of the numbers that do exist is limited in four ways:

  • These are press releases, not a statistical series. The figures are running totals "as of" a date, and the media often report a 1 December figure as the year's total. The 2024 figure circulates both as 18,035 (to 1 December) and as "more than 19.7 thousand" (the full year) [5] [4].
  • Some splits are partial. For 2022 and 2024, the origin shares only run to 1 December, so Figure 1 is partly an estimate.
  • The age shares have no clear denominator. They describe "ethnic migrants" as of a date, without saying whether that means one year's arrivals or a longer period.
  • Status is not presence. Since 2023, people can obtain kandas status through Kazakh embassies without entering the country [45], so a count of status grants is not the same as a count of children in classrooms.

These numbers are produced for a labour and resettlement purpose, so they describe kandas as labour supply: working age, education, employment, relocation. The university quota is tracked because it is a policy lever. The silence about children in school is therefore not a technical accident. It reflects which ministry owns the question, and no ministry currently owns the kandas child in a classroom.

3

Current state of research

Four research traditions each see part of the problem. None of them has tested a way to solve it.

Dossanova and Abdikadyr (2021) interviewed school-age repatriate children and described their adaptation in stages, with language the hardest of them [14]. Dossanova et al. (2026) scaled this up to a mixed-methods study of 250 pupils plus 35 interviews. Oral Kazakh was stronger than literacy, Russian and Cyrillic were barriers, and support from family, teachers and peers helped. The authors call for "targeted language-bridging programs" [13]. At university level, Valieva et al. (2019) found that adaptation problems lowered oralman students' motivation in English classes, and that no extra courses were organised for them [15]. Globally, Cummins argues that literacy built in one language transfers to the next [23], which suggests building on what children can already read rather than starting them from zero.

Where it falls short: proficiency is self-reported, the samples are small and drawn from a few cities, there is no comparison with local pupils, and there are no test-score data. The 2026 study appeared in a smaller journal (International Journal of Body, Mind and Culture) and reports no percentages.

Bokayev (2013) analyses the culture shock and stereotypes repatriates meet, and how Kazakhstani society draws "insider/outsider" lines around them [17]. Saiken's thesis on Xinjiang Kazakhs finds a hybrid identity, with skills gained in China used as capital in Kazakhstan [18]. A 2024 Frontiers in Sociology study of students from Mongolia and China shows that family ties across borders shape how they adapt [19]. Comparative research on ethnic return migration finds the same paradox from Germany to Japan: returnees are admitted for their sameness and then marginalised for their difference [22].

What it means for this project: language marks who belongs. A tool that "corrects" a child's Kazakh can deepen the very outsider label it is meant to remove.

Oka (2013) traces how the repatriation policy's priorities shifted and how its central dilemma grew [20]. Cerny (2010) shows the environmental and political pressures that pushed Kazakh herders out of China, and why Kazakhstan looked like the place "where the grass is greener" [21]. This literature treats kandas as a nation-building and demographic project.

Where it falls short: children appear mainly as numbers, which mirrors the data silence in Section 2.

A randomised trial of the Mindspark adaptive software in Delhi found large gains in maths and Hindi because it taught each child at their actual level [24]. Google's Read Along used speech recognition as a reading tutor in a pilot run with India's ASER Centre [25]. A World Bank trial in Nigeria found that a GPT-4 tutor, used inside a teacher-led after-school programme, raised English scores by 0.23 standard deviations in six weeks [26]. UNICEF's evaluations of the Akelius course show digital language learning supporting refugee children in Greece and Lebanon [27] [28].

What is known: technology helps when it targets the child's level and sits inside a structured programme run by a teacher. Used on its own, as a device handed out, it disappoints.

What is contested: which language to bridge to

Dossanova et al. treat Russian as a major barrier [13]. Raimova (2026), studying repatriate families, finds the opposite priority: Kazakh first, English second, with Russian widely rejected, often after experiences of linguistic discrimination. She calls for consistent enforcement of language policy in Russian-dominant cities [16]. This is a political choice, not a technical one. A tool that teaches Russian accommodates Russian's dominance in cities; a tool that ignores Russian leaves children stranded where Russian dominates. A second disagreement sits underneath the first: is the problem the child's language, or the school's inflexibility?

The gap this project speaks to

These literatures do not meet. The school studies describe the barrier but test no intervention. The identity studies warn about the burden of adaptation but say little about classroom practice. The technology studies show what works with refugees and struggling readers elsewhere, but none of it has been tried with kandas pupils. Nazarbayev University's open Kazakh speech corpus makes Kazakh speech recognition feasible [29], but it was not built from children or diaspora dialects. My project addresses that gap: it designs a script-bridging reading tutor and gives an honest account of whether it should exist.

4

The AI-assisted solution, and what it would cost

Көпір means "bridge". It is a reading tutor that starts from the script a child already knows.

What it is. A reading tutor on shared school tablets for kandas pupils in their first two years in a Kazakh school, used during lessons with a language-support teacher. Here is how it works:

  1. The child reads aloud

    A short Kazakh text in Cyrillic, chosen for their level.

  2. The tablet listens

    A speech-recognition model on the tablet compares what it hears with the text and finds the words the child could not decode. The audio never leaves the tablet.

    The only AI step

  3. A bridge appears

    The difficult word is also shown in a script the child already knows (tote zhazu or Latin), with a picture. Dialect pronunciations count as correct.

  4. Stories from home

    Texts are written with teachers and kandas families, including stories from the places the children come from. A teacher checks any AI-drafted text first, and there is no open chat.

  5. The teacher decides

    Each week the support teacher sees which letters and sounds each child is stuck on and plans small-group lessons. The tablet never gives grades or labels.

"school"

Run by whom, reaching whom. The Ministry of Enlightenment and regional education departments would own it. A Kazakhstani team would build it by fine-tuning Nazarbayev University's open Kazakh speech models [29], hosted on national infrastructure. It would go to the schools that receive the most kandas: in Almaty region, Mangystau, Astana, Almaty, Shymkent and Turkestan. Any pupil who struggles with reading could use it, so that it never becomes "the kandas tablet". The Russian module would be optional, chosen by each school together with families.

Why AI at all? Only step 2 needs AI, and it is the step teachers cannot scale: listening to every child read aloud, word by word, every day. Everything else (the texts, pictures and scripts) could be a printed workbook. That honesty matters for the bill.

Two documented cases

India, 2018–2019

Read Along (Bolo), a speech-based reading tutor

Google's app has an on-screen helper that listens as children read aloud and corrects them. It works offline and keeps personal data on the device. In a pilot in 200 villages in Unnao district run with the ASER Centre, 64% of children with the app improved their reading level [25].

Caveat: this was the company's own pilot, not a peer-reviewed trial.

Nigeria, 2024

A GPT-4 English tutor inside a teacher-led programme

A World Bank randomised trial in nine public secondary schools in Edo State ran twelve 90-minute after-school sessions in which pupils worked in pairs with Microsoft Copilot under teacher guidance. English scores rose by 0.23 standard deviations in six weeks [26].

Lesson: the gains came inside a structure that teachers ran.

Relevant, but not AI

UNICEF–Akelius and Mindspark

The Akelius digital language course has been used by more than 10,500 refugee children in Greece [27] and by 7,237 students in Lebanon [28]. Mindspark's adaptive software produced large gains in Delhi [24]. They show that the non-AI parts work too, which is a reason to keep the AI part small.

The bill

Change the assumptions and the bill recalculates. Every input is my assumption unless it has a source.

Fixed assumptions: 170 school days; a tablet draws 5 W; each server request uses 5× Google's published 0.24 Wh median [40], because a small national deployment will be less efficient; 0.9 kg CO₂ per kWh as a rough coal-heavy grid factor; data-centre water at 1.15 L per kWh [40]; tablets at 100,000 ₸ each; building the system and collecting a child-speech dataset costs 250 million ₸; running costs are 60 million ₸ a year; teacher training costs 50,000 ₸ per teacher, with one teacher per 15 pupils; 510 ₸ = $1. These are order-of-magnitude assumptions, not price quotes.

Көпір, year one

10,000 pupils, 20 min a day

Environmental24 MWh

Kazakhstan's electricity is still mostly coal: 62.1% of generation according to the Energy Ministry [38], while Samruk-Energy reports 74.9% from coal-fired plants in 2024 [39]. The sources disagree, which is itself a data-trust problem.

Fine-tuning the speech model once (about 500 GPU-hours) adds roughly 0.5 MWh. The larger footprint is physical: thousands of tablets made abroad and replaced every few years, plus the cooling water used by coal plants, which nobody in this chain measures.

Economic680 m ₸

Who pays: the state budget, through the Ministry of Enlightenment. Who profits: tablet manufacturers (most likely abroad), the local developer and the state cloud operator. The real comparison: the same money could pay language-support teachers. The tool is only worth buying if it multiplies those teachers' reach.

Labourshifts, not savings

Work that changes: support and class teachers get a dashboard and new duties. Without paid time, that becomes unpaid overtime.

Work that could disappear: budget substitution. A district could buy tablets instead of hiring support teachers.

Hidden labour: the speech model depends on people transcribing hours of children's speech in diaspora dialects. Nazarbayev University's corpus was itself built from more than 2,000 speakers [43]. This should be paid work for kandas adults who read tote zhazu, not unpaid help from parents. Someone also has to update the texts, fix errors and replace broken tablets.

Political and governancemedium risk

Kazakhstan's Law on Artificial Intelligence (signed 17 November 2025, in force 18 January 2026) [32] sorts systems into minimal, medium and high risk [33]. Көпір would most likely be medium risk: a failure would not cause an emergency, but it could cause moral harm to a child. It must stay at low autonomy, meaning a human makes every decision about grades, classes and placement [34]. The law also prohibits inferring emotions without consent and exploiting age-related vulnerabilities [34], so Көпір would have no emotion tracking and no design meant to maximise screen time.

Personal data must be stored in Kazakhstan [35], and the Digital Code, in force since 11 July 2026, tightens localisation further [36] [37]. The biggest misuse risk is a central list of children by country of origin. For families who left Xinjiang, such a database could feel like surveillance, and it could be used for profiling, so the central system should hold no origin field at all. The ministry would control the system, with independent oversight by the Commissioner for Children's Rights and parent representatives.

The relevant frameworks are UNESCO's Recommendation on the Ethics of AI, which Kazakhstan has been assessing itself against through UNESCO's Readiness Assessment since January 2026 [42], and UNICEF's Guidance on AI and Children 3.0 [41].

Data ethicsconsent ×2

Data needed: in daily use, voice is processed on the tablet and deleted, and only reading-progress records are kept, plus an optional question about script background. Training the model needs a separate, consented set of recordings.

Consent: the Personal Data Law requires consent from the person or their legal representative [35]. That means parents' consent plus the child's own agreement, with forms in Kazakh Cyrillic, tote zhazu, Uzbek and Chinese, and the right to refuse without losing support.

What could go wrong: (1) Dialect bias. The model hears a Xinjiang pronunciation as a mistake and tells the child their Kazakh is wrong; that is the identity harm from Section 3, automated. (2) Function creep. Reading data gets reused for migration control. (3) Stigma. Only kandas children use the tablets.

Cost per pupil, year one68,000 ₸

About 150 kandas housing certificates. The state pays up to 4.56 million ₸ per family [12].

✓

The verdict: only under these conditions

Көпір is cheap in electricity and moderate in money. It is expensive in trust and identity. Switch the conditions on and off to see how the answer changes.

No

With none of the conditions met, Көпір would be a tablet that tells returnee children their Kazakh is wrong. Kazakhstan should not build it.

My verdict is a conditional yes. The first three conditions are non-negotiable: without them, the answer is no. Kazakhstan should pilot Көпір in a small number of schools, with support teachers first and the tablet second, and it should be ready to switch the tablet off. The bill shows that the main risk is not cost. It is that the tool shifts the burden of adapting onto the child while letting the school off the hook. If the state is only willing to buy tablets, it should not do this at all.
