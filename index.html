ІзMaking sure a call for help leaves a trace

Half of the women in Kazakhstan who experience violence from a partner have never told anyone, and fewer than one in ten has told the police. When police do come, what happens next can depend on one officer at the door. This project asks why, maps what the data cannot see, and prices an AI tool called Із ("trace" in Kazakh) before deciding whether Kazakhstan should build it.

Start with the problem
1

The problem

Violence at home is common and rarely reported. When it is reported, the response often depends on a single officer.

80+women killed each year (estimate)

Kazakhstan's only nationally representative survey of violence against women interviewed 14,340 women in 2015. It found that 17% of women who had ever had a partner had experienced physical or sexual violence from him, 21% emotional abuse, and 33% controlling behaviour [1, pp. 15, 18]. A General Prosecutor's Office official estimates that at least 80 women die from domestic violence each year [10], and in December 2025 the Interior Ministry reported 96,194 people held liable that year, up 11.2% on 2024 [4].

7.8%told the police

Most of it never reaches the system. In that survey, of women who had experienced partner violence, 51% had told no one, only 7.8% had told the police, 2.9% a doctor and fewer than 0.1% an NGO or women's organisation [1, p. 80]. Abuse is still often unreported in Turkestan, Zhambyl and parts of the west [11], and in 2024 rural women were almost three times as likely as urban women to say a husband can be justified in beating his wife (6.2% against 2.2%) [2, p. 230].

What women have met at the door

  • RefusedPolice refused to register complaints.
  • Not toldWomen were not informed of their right to a protection order.
  • ReconcileWomen were urged to drop complaints and reconcile.

Human Rights Watch interviews [6]. Local police and community leaders still sometimes treat reporting as a family disgrace [11].

The law has changed fast. After the murder of Saltanat Nukenova, laws signed on 15 April 2024 made battery and minor bodily harm crimes again, allowed prosecution without the victim's complaint, and removed reconciliation in repeated battery cases [8]. Police must respond to every report [13, Art. 72] and register and investigate domestic violence cases even without a complaint [7]. Then the 2009 domestic violence law was repealed and folded into a general Law on the Prevention of Offences [13, Art. 85], and advocates fear domestic violence will be dissolved among other offences [12].

  1. 2009Law on the Prevention of Domestic Violence
  2. 2017Battery decriminalised
  3. 2024Battery and minor harm crimes again, from 16 June [9]
  4. 2025Specialised police units [16]; new prevention law adopted, 30 December
  5. 2026Merged law in force, 2 March; the 2009 law ends [13]

The law now says police must respond to every report. So why does protection still depend on whether one officer, at one door, decides to write anything down, and who pays when he doesn't?

The social-science question inside the issue

This is a question about institutions and power more than individual cruelty. At the door, the abuser is often in the room, the woman may be under pressure from family, and the officer has wide discretion. Calls to 102 are logged automatically [24], but a decision to do nothing is hard to check. When prosecutors began texting applicants about decisions in 2018, they quickly found reports of crimes "written off" instead of registered [25]. Research in Kazakhstan describes a culture of shame, ұят (uyat), that keeps women silent [R3]. The cost falls first on women and children.

Background and data

Kazakhstan now counts many things about domestic violence. What it does not publish is what happened after the call.

Prevalence survey, 2015

Statistics agency with UN Women, UNFPA and WHO. The only national measure of how many women experience violence [1].

MICS, 2024

Bureau of National Statistics with UNICEF. Attitudes only, women 15–49 and men 15–24 [2].

Interior Ministry

People held liable, arrests, protective orders, mostly via press statements [4] [5].

Prosecutor General

Legal statistics on crimes and offences, compiled by the Human Rights Commissioner [3] [15].

90,000+held liable, Jan–Sep 2026

What the data shows. Silence is the norm (Figure 1). Recorded numbers move with law and policing as much as with violence (Figures 2 and 3). Enforcement grows: police issued 85,035 protective orders in 2024 [3], and in January–September 2026 more than 90,000 people were held liable, 18.4% more than a year earlier [5]. The norms behind silence vary sharply (Figure 4): 9.0% of women in the poorest households accept wife-beating, against 1.3% in the richest [2, p. 230].

Who falls through

Household surveys miss women in shelters; the prevalence data were collected in 2015 and never repeated [1, p. 37]; MICS asks men only up to age 24. No public statistic shows how many reports police refused to register, or how many call-outs ended without any measure.

How far to trust the numbers

  • Figures conflict: one widely shared article reports 34,059 people sanctioned in 2025; the ministry reports 96,194 held liable [4]. I use the ministry's, because the article does not say what it counted.
  • Definitions shift with each reform, and official tables can be internally inconsistent (Figure 3).
  • Calls fell 17% in 2025 while the number held liable rose 11% [4]: less violence, or fewer women calling? The data cannot say.

The number that matters most here, how many call-outs ended without a protective or legal measure and why, is not public. Із is built around that gap.

3

Current state of research

Researchers have measured the violence, listened to survivors, studied institutions and tested technology, but rarely the moment police arrive.

The 2015 survey measured prevalence [1]; the 2024 MICS tracks attitudes, with sharp gaps by region, wealth and education [2]. Kainazarova and colleagues (2026) surveyed 395 adults and 29 crisis-centre staff: only 34% said they would report domestic violence, and 79.3% of staff said services lacked resources. Among the factors that keep cases hidden, they list the neglect of crimes that were reported but never registered [R1, pp. 222, 225].

Limits: self-reported, one-off data. Surveys show how much is hidden, not what happens when a woman reports.

Akhmetova, Durrani and Karabay (2025) interviewed 12 survivors and 12 professionals and show how ұят, shame, shapes how women respond to violence and how police and professionals treat them [R3]. Karabay, Akhmetova and Durrani (2024) studied professionals in violence-against-women organisations during COVID-19, and show how a crisis stretched the services survivors depend on [R2].

Limits: small samples reached through NGOs. They explain silence, but rarely follow what happens once police are involved.

In Kyrgyzstan, Childress and colleagues (2023) interviewed 83 professionals and found a gap between what domestic violence laws intend and what police, courts and social services do [R4]. Akisheva (2026) shows that Central Asian laws still fall short of CEDAW and the Beijing Declaration, and that Kazakhstan does not treat domestic violence as a distinct crime even after 2024 [R5]. A 2024 meta-analysis of 34 studies found that arrest does not consistently reduce repeat violence [R6].

What follows: tougher laws alone do not protect. What matters is a consistent, accountable response.

In Spain, a study of 7,147 police reports found that VioGén's risk form identified 79% of women later attacked again, but also flagged many who were not (specificity 35%, AUC 0.62) [R8]. On Greater Manchester Police data, machine-learning models based on criminal histories beat the standard police questionnaire [R9]. In Almaty, women who use drugs, sell sex or live with HIV who used the UMAI-WINGS phone programme were 29% less likely to report physical partner violence six months later than a control group [R7].

Limits: none has been tested in Kazakhstan's police practice, and most measure prediction, not whether women ended up safer.

What is contested

Do arrest and criminalisation protect women?

Kazakhstan's 2024 reform assumes tougher penalties help. The international evidence is mixed [R6].

Do risk algorithms protect women, or ration protection?

VioGén's designers point to its ability to find women at risk [R8]. An audit reports, citing a 2014 study, that officers kept the automatic score in 95% of cases, and that in 2021 only 1 in 7 women who sought police protection got it [C1, pp. 13, 34].

The gap

No study I found measures what happens at the moment of police contact in Kazakhstan: how often reports are refused or closed without action, and whether recorded decisions change that.

Kainazarova et al. name unregistered reports as a cause but cannot count them [R1]. No risk or survivor tool has been tested in Kazakh or Russian, inside Kazakhstan's legal system. Із is a design for making that moment visible.

4

The AI-assisted solution

Із has two connected parts: one for the woman, one for the officer at her door.

Part 1, for survivors, run by crisis centres

Record in seconds, decide later

  • 10-second capture: one tap for a photo, a voice note in Kazakh or Russian, or a line of text, dated automatically.
  • Hidden and safe: a neutral icon, quick exit, nothing in the gallery, encrypted storage in Kazakhstan that survives a taken phone.
  • AI works at once: each entry is transcribed, dated and placed on a timeline, and a correctly worded заявление (police report) is drafted for her to review when she is safe.
  • Rights in plain words: what police must do, and where to complain if they refuse.
  • She decides: nothing is shared unless she chooses, directly or through a crisis-centre lawyer.

Part 2, for police

A decision that is written down

  • A structured assessment at the call-out, on a tablet, linked to the logged 102 call.
  • An AI risk estimate from a model trained and validated on past Kazakh cases, combining the answers with case history [R9].
  • The legal options on screen, not a verdict.
  • A recorded reason: if the officer takes no measure, he must write why. High risk with no measure is flagged automatically.
  • She gets a copy of the decision.

Who checks the decision

Individual cases, to bodies with power

  1. Supervisor reviews every flagged case.
  2. Prosecutor must check that reports are received and registered lawfully, and cancels unlawful decisions [15].
  3. Investigating court hears her complaint if a report is refused [14].

Patterns, anonymised

  1. Closure rates by district, repeat addresses, flagged cases with no measure.
  2. Sent to the Human Rights Commissioner and parliament.
  3. Published in an annual public report.

The automatic flag is my proposal; the powers it relies on already exist.

Where AI adds value, and where it does not

Timestamped, encrypted evidenceNo AI needed
Recording every decision and linking it to the 102 callNo AI needed
Flagging high risk with no measureA rule, not AI
Transcribing Kazakh and Russian voice notesAI
Turning fragments into a timeline and a draft заявлениеAI
Estimating risk from case historiesAI, if validated [R9]

The pilot should compare current procedure, Із without AI and Із with AI, and keep AI only where it adds benefit without harming women's safety or rights. Past records reflect who was registered, not who was at risk, so two rules apply: a low score can never justify withholding a measure the law requires, and the model is tested on women with little recorded history.

Two documented cases

Spain, since 2007

VioGén: national police risk assessment

Spain's Interior Ministry system scores a police risk questionnaire with an algorithm and links the score to protection measures. It has run more than 3 million risk evaluations [C1, p. 11]; its form caught 79% of women later re-victimised, but correctly cleared only 35% of those who were not (AUC 0.62) [R8].

Lesson: officers kept the automatic score in 95% of cases (a 2014 study cited by the audit), and 35% of women the auditors interviewed had not been told their score [C1, pp. 13, 25]. A score can replace judgement instead of supporting it.

Five EU countries, 2025

AinoAid: an AI chatbot for survivors

A chatbot giving information on support services, risk and legal procedures. Its evaluation interviewed 80 survivors in five countries and, separately, surveyed 669 users in Germany, who rated it positively, perceived safety most of all [R10].

Limit: it measured usability and perceived safety, not whether violence decreased.

The bill

An illustrative scenario, not a procurement estimate. Change the assumptions and the bill recalculates; every input without a source is my own assumption.

Defaults: assessments match the more than 84,000 protective orders of 2025 [4]; officers match the specialised units' 1,000 staff [16].

My assumptions: three AI requests per entry or assessment, each 5× Google's measured 0.24 Wh and 0.26 mL of water [21]; four 0.5 kW storage and app servers all year (Google's figure already covers inference overhead); 10 kWh per tablet; 0.9 kg CO₂/kWh. Build 350m ₸, running 100m ₸ and audit 30m ₸ a year; tablet 150,000 ₸, training 60,000 ₸ per officer; 510 ₸ = $1.

Із, year one

Environmental

Kazakhstan's electricity is mostly coal: 62.1% according to the Energy Ministry, while Samruk-Energy reports that coal plants supplied 74.9% of the unified grid in 2024 [20].

Servers, not AI requests, use most of the energy. The bigger footprint is physical: tablets, and years of securely stored photos and audio.

Economic

Who pays: the state budget, through the Interior Ministry and crisis-centre funding. Who profits: the developer, the cloud operator and the tablet maker. The real comparison: the state spent almost 3 billion ₸ on crisis centres in 2024 [3]. Paid for by cutting shelters, Із would cost more than it saves.

Labourshifts, not savings

Work that changes: the specialised units were staffed by moving existing personnel, with no extra budget [16]. Paperwork without extra time risks box-ticking, and flags add review work for supervisors and prosecutors.

Hidden labour: vetted staff, not outsourced click-workers, must label sensitive case files to train the model; lawyers must update legal templates whenever the law changes; crisis-centre lawyers handle shared evidence.

The quiet shift: women do the documenting, which is fair only if the state acts on it.

Political and governancehigh risk

Kazakhstan's AI Law (No. 230-VIII, in force January 2026) sorts systems into minimal, medium and high risk, but the owner classifies its own system [18, Art. 17]. Buribayeva and colleagues warn that such risks are built in through legal design before any harm is visible [W1], so Із should declare itself high-risk and accept audit [18, Arts. 18–20].

The law also bans classifying people by predicted personal characteristics "except in cases provided for by the laws", and detecting emotions without consent [18, Art. 17(3)]. The police score therefore needs an explicit legal basis, such as an amendment to Law 245-VIII, and no voice-emotion analysis. It must be low-autonomy, which the law defines as a person always making the final choice; people affected can be told about automated processing and object to it [18, Arts. 7, 17]. Data stays in Kazakhstan [17].

The EU AI Act lists police tools that assess a person's risk of becoming a crime victim as high-risk, and the Commission's draft guidelines name domestic-violence risk assessment as an example [23]. Roberts and colleagues describe global AI governance as a weak "regime complex" [W2]: no single standard exists, so Із should meet the stricter of the two. Kazakhstan began a UNESCO readiness assessment of its AI use in January 2026 [19].

Misuse risks: an abuser finding the app; police reading her archive; records used in custody disputes; scores leaking into household profiling such as the Digital Family Card, whose errors citizens rarely see [W1]. So crisis centres, not police, run the survivor side.

Data ethicstwo kinds of consent

Data needed: her evidence; the officer's assessment and decision; past case files, including health and children's data.

Consent: her evidence is shared only with her consent. Police records are official records of a state decision, so they are reviewed without it, and pressure on her cannot stop oversight of the police. Mhlambi's relational ethics treats the harms of automated decisions as harms to relationships [W3], which fits a woman whose "no" may be her family's. He also argues that marginalised communities are experts on their own experience, so survivors and crisis-centre staff should co-design Із.

What could go wrong: missed danger [C1]; false alarms that wear officers out; and bias from the start. A model trained on records learns where women report and police register, not where violence happens.

The verdict: only under conditions

On my assumptions, Із uses little electricity and a moderate budget; its real cost is the risk of a score that rations protection. Switch the conditions to see the verdict change.

No

My verdict is a conditional yes, with the two parts treated differently. Kazakhstan should pilot the survivor side now, through crisis centres, because it puts the record in the woman's hands and costs little. It should pilot the police side only if the other three conditions also hold. Its main risk is not money but a number that makes a decision look neutral [W1] and lets an officer, or a ministry, decide someone does not need protection. If Kazakhstan wants a score that tells police whom they can leave at home, it should not build Із at all.

AI transparency statement

What AI contributed. Claude (Anthropic) helped me brainstorm ideas, searched for and checked sources and laws, drafted the text, and wrote the code for this site.

What I contributed. I chose the problem, police failing to register reports and leaving abusers at home, and developed the core idea of combining a tool for survivors with accountability for police decisions. I made the key decisions that shaped the argument:

  • The survivor tool had to work in seconds, with the AI processing each entry the moment it is sent, because a woman living with her abuser may have no time to spend on an app.
  • I rejected wording suggesting abusers should simply be detained. Every measure on the site is tied to what Kazakh law allows, and I checked the articles in the law texts.
  • The AI first proposed the Human Rights Commissioner as the main oversight body. I rejected that, because the Commissioner cannot overturn decisions, and asked for bodies with real power: the supervisor, the prosecutor and the investigating court.
  • I asked what happens if a woman is pressured to withhold consent. That is why her evidence and the police records are treated separately.
  • I dropped a widely shared figure (34,059 sanctioned in 2025) because its source did not say what it counted.

Checking. I checked every source for hallucinations, and the bibliography was also checked with ChatGPT. Figures were compared with the original survey reports, law texts and articles. This identified errors in the AI's drafts, which were corrected: among them, a preprint's 43.9% used instead of the published 29%, and a claim that no woman had told a women's organisation (the survey records fewer than 0.1%).
