PLS 419 final project: an AI answer to a social problem in Kazakhstan

23:12A neighbour calls 102. Shouting, something breaking.

23:41Two officers arrive. He is calm now. She says she is fine.

23:52They leave. No protocol, no protective order.

–Nothing from this night will appear in any statistic.

An illustrative scenario, not a real case.

ІзMaking sure a call for help leaves a trace

In Kazakhstan, half of the women who experience violence from a partner have never told anyone, and fewer than one in ten has told the police. When police do come, what happens next can depend on one officer's decision at the door. This project asks why, maps what the data can and cannot see, and prices an AI tool called Із ("trace" in Kazakh) before deciding whether Kazakhstan should build it.

Start with the problem
1

The problem

Violence at home is common and rarely reported. When it is reported, the response often depends on a single officer.

Kazakhstan's only nationally representative survey of violence against women interviewed 14,340 women aged 18 to 75. It found that 17% of women who had ever had a partner had experienced physical or sexual violence from that partner, 21% emotional abuse, and 33% at least one form of controlling behaviour [1, pp. 15, 18]. A General Prosecutor's Office official estimates that at least 80 women die from domestic violence each year [10]. The Interior Ministry held 96,194 people administratively liable for domestic offences in 2025, up 11.2% from 86,518 in 2024 [4].

The same survey shows how much never reaches the system. Of the women who had experienced partner violence, 51% had never told anyone. Only 7.8% had told the police and 2.9% a doctor, and not one named a women's organisation [1, p. 80]. The silence is also uneven. Abuse is still often unreported in more traditional regions such as Turkestan, Zhambyl and parts of the west [11], and in 2024 rural women were almost three times as likely as urban women to say that a husband can be justified in beating his wife (6.2% against 2.2%) [2, p. 230].

This project is about what happens when women do turn to the police. Human Rights Watch documented police refusing to register complaints, failing to tell women about their right to a protection order, and urging them to drop complaints and reconcile with their abusers [6]. Local police and community leaders still sometimes treat reporting as a family disgrace [11].

The law has changed fast (see the timeline). After the murder of Saltanat Nukenova, laws signed on 15 April 2024 made battery and minor bodily harm crimes again, allowed prosecution without the victim's complaint, and removed reconciliation in repeated battery cases [8]. They came into force on 16 June 2024 [9]. Police must now register and investigate every domestic violence case, even without a complaint [7], and in early 2025 the Interior Ministry set up specialised domestic violence units [16]. Then, at the end of 2025, the separate domestic violence law was repealed and folded into a general Law on the Prevention of Offences [12], in force from 2 March 2026 [13]. Advocates fear domestic violence will be dissolved among other offences.

The law now says police must respond to every report. So why does protection still depend on whether one officer, at one door, decides to write anything down, and who pays when he doesn't?

The social-science question inside the issue

This is a question about institutions and power more than about individual cruelty. At the door, the abuser is often in the room, the woman may be under pressure from family, and the officer has wide discretion. Above all, a decision to do nothing leaves no record. Research in Kazakhstan also describes a culture of shame, ұят (uyat), that keeps women silent [R3]. The cost falls on women and children first, then on officers asked to change a culture without new resources, and on the state's credibility.

2

Background and data

Kazakhstan now counts many things about domestic violence. The one thing it cannot count is a call that nobody wrote down.

Who measures what. The statistics agency ran the 2017 prevalence survey with UN Women, UNFPA and WHO. It is still the only nationally representative measure of how many women experience violence [1]. The Bureau of National Statistics' 2024 Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS), run with UNICEF and published in November 2025, asks about attitudes toward violence rather than experience of it, and only of women aged 15–49 and men aged 15–24 [2]. The Interior Ministry publishes enforcement figures [4] [5]; the Prosecutor General's legal statistics committee records crimes [15]; the Human Rights Commissioner compiles both [3].

What the data shows. Silence is the norm (Figure 1), and recorded numbers move with law and policing as much as with violence (Figure 2). Enforcement keeps growing. Police issued 85,035 protective orders in 2024 [3], and in January–September 2026 more than 90,000 people were held liable (18.4% more than a year earlier), 25,000 were placed under administrative arrest, and 20,500 were held liable for breaking protective orders or court restrictions [5]. The norms behind silence vary sharply (Figure 3). 9.0% of women in the poorest households accept wife-beating, against 1.3% in the richest, and 13.4% of women with lower secondary education, against 1.7% with higher education [2, p. 230].

Where the data is silent

Who falls through. Household surveys miss women in shelters; the prevalence figures are nine years old; MICS asks men only up to age 24. Most important here, no source records a report that police refused to register, or a call-out that ended without any measure.

How far the numbers can be trusted. Figures reach the public mostly through ministry press statements, and they conflict. One widely shared article reports 34,059 people sanctioned in 2025, while the ministry reports 96,194 held liable [4]. I use the ministry's, because the article does not say what it counted. Definitions shift with each reform. Finally, falling reports can mean less violence or fewer women calling. Reports fell 28% in the second half of 2024 [9], and calls fell 17% in 2025 while the number held liable rose by 11% [4]. The data cannot tell these apart. Prevalence estimates differ too: 17% in the 2017 survey [1], and 27% for 2023 in a World Bank figure cited by a later study [R7], with different definitions.

The number that matters most here is how many calls ended with no record, and it does not exist. Із is built around that gap.

3

Current state of research

Researchers have measured the violence, listened to survivors, studied institutions and tested technology. They have rarely studied the moment police arrive.

Quantitative work starts from the 2017 national survey [1]. Kainazarova and colleagues (2026) argue that domestic violence in Kazakhstan remains latent because of underreporting and social norms that put women at a disadvantage, so official numbers capture only part of it [R1]. The 2024 MICS tracks attitudes and finds sharp gaps by region, wealth and education [2].

Limits: self-reported, one-off data. Surveys show how much is hidden, not what happens when a woman reports.

Akhmetova, Durrani and Karabay (2025) interviewed 12 survivors and 12 professionals. They show how ұят, shame, shapes how women respond to violence and how they are treated by police and professionals [R3]. Karabay, Akhmetova and Durrani (2024) studied professionals in Kazakhstan's violence-against-women organisations during COVID-19, and show how a crisis stretched the services survivors depend on [R2].

Limits: small samples reached through NGOs. They explain silence, but rarely follow what happens once police are involved.

In Kyrgyzstan, Childress and colleagues (2023) interviewed 83 professionals and found a gap between what domestic violence laws intend and what police, courts and social services actually do [R4]. Akisheva (2026) shows that Central Asian laws still fall short of CEDAW and the Beijing Declaration, and that Kazakhstan does not treat domestic violence as a distinct crime even after 2024 [R5]. Globally, a 2024 meta-analysis of 34 studies found that arrest does not consistently reduce repeat violence [R6].

What follows: tougher laws alone do not protect. What matters is a consistent and accountable response.

In Spain, a study of 7,147 police reports found that the risk form behind the VioGén system identified 79% of women who were later attacked again, but also flagged many women who were not (specificity 35%, AUC 0.62) [R8]. On Greater Manchester Police data, machine-learning models based on criminal histories predicted repeat violence better than the standard police questionnaire [R9]. The AinoAid chatbot for survivors was rated positively by 669 users, who rated perceived safety highest [R10]. In Almaty, women who use drugs, sell sex or live with HIV who used the UMAI-WINGS phone programme were 29% less likely to report physical partner violence six months later than a control group [R7].

Limits: none of these tools has been tested inside Kazakhstan's police practice. Most measure prediction or user satisfaction, not whether women ended up safer.

What is contested

First, whether criminalisation and arrest protect women. Kazakhstan's 2024 reform assumes that tougher penalties help, while the international evidence is mixed [R6]. Second, whether risk algorithms protect women or ration protection. VioGén's designers point to its ability to identify women at risk [R8]. An independent audit reports that officers kept the automatic score in 95% of cases, and that in 2021 only 1 in 7 women who went to the police for protection received it [C1, pp. 13, 34].

The gap this project speaks to

No study I found examines what happens at the moment of police contact in Kazakhstan: how often reports are refused or closed without action, and whether structured, recorded decisions change that. Technology studies show that risk can be estimated and survivors supported elsewhere, but no such tool has been tested in Kazakh or Russian, or inside Kazakhstan's police and legal system. Із is a design for making that moment visible.

4

The AI-assisted solution

Із has two connected parts: one for the woman, one for the officer at her door.

Part 1, for survivors

Record in seconds, decide later

  • 10-second capture: one tap for a photo, a voice note in Kazakh or Russian, or a line of text, dated automatically.
  • Hidden and safe: a neutral icon, quick exit, nothing in the gallery, encrypted storage in Kazakhstan that survives a taken phone.
  • AI works at once: the moment she sends an entry, it is transcribed, dated and placed on a timeline, and a correctly worded заявление (police report) is drafted for her to review whenever she is safe.
  • Rights in plain words: what police must do, and where to complain if they refuse.
  • She decides: nothing is shared unless she chooses, directly or through a crisis-centre lawyer. A relative or neighbour can report too.

Part 2, for police

A decision that is written down

  • A structured assessment at the call-out, on a tablet, for the specialised domestic violence units.
  • An AI risk estimate: a model trained and validated on past Kazakh cases that combines the answers with case history, as in the British research [R9].
  • The legal options on screen, not a verdict: detention where the law gives grounds, a protective order, an administrative case, or a criminal report.
  • A recorded reason: if the officer takes no measure, he must write why. High risk with no measure is flagged automatically.
  • The survivor gets a copy of the decision.

Who checks the decision

Track 1: individual cases, to bodies with power

  1. Supervisor reviews every flagged case.
  2. Prosecutor is required to check that reports are received and registered lawfully, and can cancel unlawful decisions [15].
  3. Investigating court hears her complaint if a report is refused [14].

Track 2: patterns, anonymised

  1. Closure rates by district, repeat addresses, flagged cases with no measure.
  2. Sent to the Human Rights Commissioner and parliament.
  3. Published in an annual public report.

The automatic flag is my proposal; it does not exist in law today. The powers it relies on do.

Why AI, and where not. AI does two things people cannot do at scale: turning Kazakh or Russian voice and text fragments into a dated record and legal draft, and estimating risk from case histories better than a checklist [R9]. The most important part needs no AI: every decision at the door is written down.

Two documented cases

Spain, since 2007

VioGén: national police risk assessment

Spain's Interior Ministry system rates the risk faced by women who report gender violence and links it to protection measures. It has carried out more than 3 million risk evaluations [C1, p. 11], and its police form identified 79% of women later re-victimised [R8].

Lesson: officers kept the automatic score in 95% of cases, and 35% of the women the auditors interviewed had not been told their score [C1, pp. 13, 25]. A score can replace judgement instead of supporting it.

Five EU countries, 2025

AinoAid: an AI chatbot for survivors

A chatbot that gives information on support services, risk and legal procedures. Its evaluation included interviews with 80 survivors in Austria, Finland, France, Germany and Spain, and a survey of 669 users, who rated it positively, perceived safety most of all [R10].

Limit: it measured usability and perceived safety, not whether violence decreased.

The bill

Change the assumptions and the bill recalculates. Every input without a source is my own assumption.

Where the defaults come from: police assessments match the more than 84,000 protective orders issued in 2025 [4], and officers match the specialised units' 1,000 staff [16].

My assumptions: three AI requests per entry or assessment, each 5× Google's measured 0.24 Wh and 0.26 mL of water [21]; four 0.5 kW servers all year; 10 kWh per tablet; 0.9 kg CO₂/kWh. Build 350m ₸, running 100m ₸ and audit 30m ₸ a year; tablet 150,000 ₸, training 60,000 ₸ per officer; 510 ₸ = $1.

Із, year one

Environmental

Kazakhstan's electricity is mostly coal: 62.1% according to the Energy Ministry [20], while Samruk-Energy reports that coal plants supplied 74.9% of the unified grid in 2024 [20].

Servers, not AI requests, use most of the energy. The bigger footprint is physical: tablets, and years of securely stored photos and audio.

Economic

Who pays: the state budget, through the Interior Ministry for the police side and crisis-centre funding for the survivor side. Who profits: the developer, the cloud operator and the tablet maker. The real comparison: the state spent almost 3 billion ₸ on crisis centres in 2024 [3]. If Із were paid for by cutting shelters, it would cost more than it saves.

Labourshifts, not savings

Work that changes: the specialised units, about 1,000 officers, were staffed by moving existing personnel, with no extra budget [16]. Paperwork without extra time risks box-ticking, and flags add review work for supervisors and prosecutors.

Hidden labour: vetted staff, not outsourced click-workers, must label sensitive past case files to train the model; lawyers must update legal templates whenever the law changes (2024, 2026); crisis-centre lawyers handle shared evidence.

The quiet shift: the app moves work onto women, who now do the documenting. That is only fair if the state then acts on what they record.

Political and governancehigh risk

Kazakhstan's Law on Artificial Intelligence came into force on 18 January 2026 [18]. It sorts systems by risk and classes as high risk those whose failure could threaten citizens' lives and health [18]. The police tool belongs there. It must stay at low autonomy, with a human making every decision, and the law bars inferring emotions without consent, so there is no voice-emotion analysis [18]. Personal data must be stored in Kazakhstan [17].

Misuse risks: an abuser finding the app; police reading her archive; records used against her in custody disputes; profiling of districts. So crisis centres, not police, run the survivor side, and her evidence stays separate from police records. The design follows UNESCO's Recommendation on the Ethics of AI. In January 2026 Kazakhstan began a UNESCO readiness assessment of its own AI use [19].

Data ethicstwo kinds of consent

Data needed: her evidence; the officer's assessment and decision; past case files, including health and children's data.

Consent: her evidence is shared only with her consent. Police records are official records of a state decision, so they are reviewed without her consent. Pressure on her therefore cannot stop oversight of the police.

What could go wrong: missed danger, as the VioGén audit reports [C1]; false alarms that wear officers out; and a bias built in from the start. A model trained on records learns where women report and police register, not where violence happens, so it could rate risk lowest where women report least.

The verdict: only under conditions

Із is cheap in electricity and moderate in money; its real cost is the risk of a score that rations protection. Switch the conditions to see the verdict change.

No

My verdict is a conditional yes, with the two parts treated differently. Kazakhstan should pilot the survivor side now, run by crisis centres, because it puts the record in the woman's hands and costs little. It should pilot the police side only if the other three conditions also hold, because its main risk is not money but a number that lets an officer, or a ministry, decide someone does not need protection. If what Kazakhstan wants is a score that tells police whom they can leave at home, it should not build Із at all.

AI transparency statement

What AI contributed. I worked with Claude (Anthropic) throughout. It helped me compare possible topics, searched for and checked sources and laws, extracted figures from the two survey reports, drafted the text, and wrote the code for this site, including the charts, the bill calculator and the verdict switch.

What I rejected or changed, and why.

  • I dropped an earlier topic on returnee (kandas) children's literacy. The evidence could not show how many children actually knew the Arabic-based script, and transliteration does not need AI. I wanted a problem I care about that genuinely needs AI.
  • I required the survivor tool to work in seconds, because a woman living with her abuser may not have time to spend on an app, and I asked for the AI to process each entry the moment it is sent so she can review it later.
  • I rejected wording suggesting abusers should simply be detained. Every measure on this site is tied to what Kazakh law actually allows.
  • The AI first proposed the Human Rights Commissioner as the main oversight body. I rejected that because the Commissioner cannot overturn decisions and asked for bodies with real power. That led to the supervisor, the prosecutor and the investigating court, which we then checked in the law.
  • I asked what happens if a woman is pressured to withhold consent. That is why her own evidence and the police records are treated separately.
  • I asked for newer research and limited the academic sources to ten, which replaced several older studies.
  • I dropped a widely shared figure (34,059 sanctioned in 2025) because its source did not say what it counted.
  • Checking caught errors in the AI's drafts: it had used a preprint's 43.9% figure instead of the published 29%, and had misread the VioGén audit's 35% (women not told their score, not women who knew it). Both are corrected.

What I accepted. Combining a survivor tool with a police tool, using VioGén and AinoAid as case studies, the two-track oversight design, and the cost assumptions, which are labelled as assumptions.

Checking. Every statistic links to its source. Figures from the two survey reports give the PDF page, so anyone can check them.
